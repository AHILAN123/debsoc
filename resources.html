Section 01

What does DebSoc mean?

DebSoc is short for Debating Society. You will also hear it called the Debating Club: the club and the society are one and the same. Plenty of colleges have one, so the word alone does not tell you which. The one this site belongs to is the Debating Society of IIEST Shibpur, usually written DebSoc IIEST. It is based in Howrah, West Bengal, and it is not to be confused with any other college’s DebSoc.

A debating club is a student-run group that meets regularly to practise arguing, speaking in public and, less obviously, listening. Most also send members to tournaments and run a few of their own. That is the whole definition. The rest is culture: who stays back to help a nervous first-year with a speech, and who argues with the chair about a point of order.

DebSoc IIEST covers parliamentary debate, Oxford-style debate, Model United Nations, and elocution and other speech competitions. Members can do any of them, or all of them. The club has been active since 2009. It runs weekly sessions in term, usually after classes, and takes in new members each year through an induction called Disputation. It also hosts BECON MUN. You do not need experience to join.

Section 02

What is MUN (Model United Nations)?

Model United Nations is a simulation. Students take the role of delegates, usually representing countries, and sit in a mock committee of the United Nations. The committee has an agenda, say, regulating satellite-based weapons, and the delegates spend a weekend arguing, negotiating and trying to write either a resolution or a consensus document that the whole room will vote for or adopt.

A conference is split into committees. Each has a chair or an Executive Board who runs the room, enforces procedure and scores delegates. In practice the Executive Board is your judge. Committees might copy a real body, such as the General Assembly, the Security Council or the Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC). Others are specialised agencies, such as the International Labour Organization (ILO), and some are not UN bodies at all: a national parliament, a cabinet, a historical crisis. BECON MUN 2026 ran a UN committee and a national one, UNGA-DISEC and the Lok Sabha.

Conferences usually last two or three days and give out awards at the end. What is on offer is research, public speaking, negotiating with people who want something different from you, and drafting under time pressure. Very little of it is about being the loudest person in the room, whatever it looks like from outside.

Format, rules and awards vary by conference. Some are heavy on formal speeches, others on informal negotiation. Read the conference’s own rules and background guide before you arrive.

Section 03

What does a delegate do in MUN?

A delegate speaks for a country (or a person, in a parliament or cabinet committee), not for themselves. Your job is to work out what that country wants, argue for it, and find other delegates who want something close enough to build a deal with.

Before the conference

Read the background guide. Research your country’s past votes, statements and treaties on the agenda. Then research the committee itself: what it is allowed to do, what it is not, and which other UN bodies it works with. Write a position paper if the conference asks for one, and prepare a short opening speech.

In the room

  • Answer roll call and take part in setting the agenda.
  • Give speeches from the speakers’ list, and speak during the formal-informal sessions.
  • Use the informal time to find allies and write a working paper together.
  • Vote on amendments and on the final draft resolution, where the committee has one.

What separates good delegates from busy ones

Good delegates spend their time understanding how the committee actually works. Not just how one country works, but how this UN body works inside the wider UN system. ECOSOC, for example, does not only talk to member states. It coordinates the UN’s specialised agencies, so it can make recommendations to a body like the ILO. Knowing that is what lets you write a clause that does something.

In a consensus-based committee, such as ECOSOC or the GA’s economic and financial committee (ECOFIN), ask which bodies this committee liaises with and what each of them can deliver. Clauses do not always have to be addressed to member states.

Compare these two clauses:

Weak: “Recommends that Member States donate more.”

Better: “Recommends that the ILO, in coordination with ECOSOC, negotiate better terms with all stakeholders in line with just energy transition principles, while requesting Member States to provide grants instead of concessional loans, when and however applicable.”

The first creates no idea anyone can act on. The second names who acts, through which channel, on what principle, and with what money. Phrases like “when and however applicable” keep a clause flexible enough for the room to accept it.

Speaking often only helps if you know what the bodies at large are doing. Chairs notice delegates who understand the UN system and build a mechanism into their clauses. They also notice the bland ones.

Section 04

MUN rules of procedure: a beginner’s walkthrough

Rules of procedure are what stop forty delegates from talking at once. Most committees DebSoc members sit in follow UN4MUN procedure, which the UN designed to mirror how its own meetings actually run. It is built around consultation and consensus rather than winning votes. Conferences still change the details, and the conference’s own rules always win.

UN4MUN splits committee time into three kinds of session:

  1. Formal-formal. The formal meeting, with the chair running a speakers’ list. Opening speeches happen here, and the committee comes back here at the end to adopt its document.
  2. Formal-informal. Discussion still run by the chair, but looser. Delegates debate specific parts of the agenda and the draft, and this is where most of the speaking happens.
  3. Informal-informal. No chair and no rules: delegates leave their seats to lobby, negotiate and write together.

The committee moves back and forth between formal-informal and informal-informal as the draft develops. The aim is a document the whole room can adopt by consensus. A vote is the exception, not the goal.

Committees are academic, so you may also be asked to do academic work: present a PPT on a particular topic, or write a short report. Treat these as part of the committee, not extra homework.

Some conferences use a different, older style (often called UNA-USA procedure) with moderated and unmoderated caucuses and many more motions. The glossary covers those terms in case you meet them.

Do not try to memorise any rulebook. Learn the three kinds of session, and ask the chair about anything else. Chairs would rather answer a question than untangle a mistake.

Section 05

How to write a MUN position paper

A position paper is a short document, usually one or two pages, that tells the chairs where your country stands on the agenda. Many conferences ask for one before the event and some score it. Check the conference’s length, format and deadline, because they differ.

Keep it simple. A heading block (committee, country, delegate names, topic) is followed by three parts:

  1. The issue. The problem, in plain terms. Pick the part of it your country cares about most.
  2. The solution, through the UN system. What the committee should do, and through which body or programme. Read the UN’s own policy recommendations on the topic and build on them.
  3. Where it has worked. At the end, cite a precedent in one line: the country, and in brackets, what was done. Do not go deeper than that.

You do not need to name every national scheme your country has run. The chairs can look that up. What they cannot look up is whether you understand how the committee could solve the problem.

The shape of a good paper

(1) [The issue in two or three sentences, and the specific part of it your country will focus on.]

(2) [The recommendation: which UN body should act, with whom, and how. For example, a recommendation to a specialised agency, made in coordination with this committee.]

(3) [One-line precedent: “A similar approach was applied in [country] ([what was done]).”]

Section 06

How to give an opening speech in MUN

An opening speech is your first turn on the speakers’ list. The point is to tell the room what the problem is and which direction your country will take on it. It is not the place to recite your whole position paper.

First, understand the committee

Before you write a word, work out what the committee can do and what it cannot. A speech that asks a committee for something outside its mandate tells the chair you have not done that work.

Pick one part of the problem and hammer it

Do not bring up five topics. Choose one specific part of the problem, state it in the simplest terms you can, and stay on it. In a Commission on the Status of Women (CSW) committee, that might be customary laws and practices, and the difficulty of litigating against them. You might ask which countries are involved, what the UNDP has already done, and how ECOSOC could support that work. In ECOSOC, it might be which ILO conventions apply to workers in new technologies, and how the transition to AI will affect them.

Then lay out the line your country will hold until the end of committee. If you will mostly argue the labour perspective in ECOSOC, or the tokenistic representation of women in positions of power, say so in the opening speech. Everything you say later should build on it.

An example of citing a specific problem

This is the kind of point that works, in a speech or in formal-informal. Some states have used counter-terrorism laws as a blanket ban on aid, legal, food and medical, to people in areas held by groups they call rebels or terrorists. Syria’s 2012 counter-terrorism law, for example, was used to prosecute people for delivering aid to opposition-held areas. International humanitarian law, by contrast, requires parties to a conflict to allow and facilitate rapid, unimpeded humanitarian relief for civilians in need. So the committee can call on states in that situation to comply, and build political pressure on them to do so.

That point fits a CSW or a UN Women committee, because women need medical support in childbirth and need gender-based violence kits. It fits ECOSOC whenever aid comes up. And it is built entirely on legal understanding, which is why it lands. Check the law yourself before you use an argument like this.

What not to do

  • Do not open with a greeting. “Honourable Chair, fellow delegates” is stock MUN jargon. Some circuits tolerate it. In Kolkata it will cost you. Open with the problem, stated as a fact.
  • Do not ask the room a question. “I would like to ask every delegate here…” wastes your time. Tell them something.
  • Do not list proposals. Name the issue and the direction your country is going in. The detail comes in formal-informal.
  • Do not invite people into your bloc. They will come anyway if your line is clear.

Going a few seconds over time is not a disaster, but know your limit, because the chair can still cut you off.

Section 07

What is Asian Parliamentary debate?

Asian Parliamentary (AP) is a debate format with two teams of three speakers each. One team is Government (also called Proposition) and defends the motion. The other is Opposition and argues against it. Teams are told which side they have, and are given the motion shortly before the debate starts, typically with around 30 minutes to prepare. That is a common pattern, not a universal one.

The six speakers

  • Prime Minister: defines the motion, sets out the Government case and gives its first arguments.
  • Leader of the Opposition: rebuts and presents the Opposition case.
  • Deputy Prime Minister and Deputy Leader of the Opposition: rebuild their own side, rebut the other, and add new arguments.
  • Government Whip and Opposition Whip: do not bring new arguments. They compare the two sides and say why theirs won the debate.

Substantive speeches commonly run around seven minutes. After them each side gives a short reply speech, often about four minutes, delivered by the first or second speaker; the Opposition replies first. Points of information (see section 09) may be offered between the protected first and last minutes of a substantive speech, though protected time and timings differ by tournament.

AP rewards clear structure and direct clash. The usual yardstick is that every speech should answer “what did the other side just say, and why does it matter less than what I am saying?”

Section 08

British Parliamentary debate: the four teams and their roles

British Parliamentary (BP) puts four teams of two in one room, all debating the same motion at the same time. Two teams are on the Government bench and two are on the Opposition bench. At the end the judges rank all four, first to fourth, instead of picking one winner and one loser.

The four teams

  • Opening Government (OG): Prime Minister and Deputy Prime Minister. They define the motion and open the debate.
  • Opening Opposition (OO): Leader of the Opposition and Deputy Leader. They respond and set up the opposing case.
  • Closing Government (CG): Member for the Government and Government Whip.
  • Closing Opposition (CO): Member for the Opposition and Opposition Whip.

The closing teams have a harder job than it sounds. They are on the same side as the opening team on their bench, but they are competing against them for ranking. So they must bring an extension: a new, distinct line of argument that the opening team did not cover. Repeating the opening team’s case, however well, tends to rank you lower than a team that added something. The Whips then summarise their bench’s debate without introducing new arguments.

Speeches are commonly around seven minutes, and the teams usually get about 15 minutes to prepare after seeing the motion. Points of information are offered by the opposite bench, again outside the protected first and last minute. As with AP, details vary between tournaments, so read the rules for the one you enter.

Section 09

What is a POI (Point of Information)?

A POI is a short question or challenge that a speaker on the other side offers while you are speaking. They stand and say “point of information” or “on that point”, and you decide whether to accept it. If you accept, they get about fifteen seconds. If you decline, they sit down and you carry on.

In parliamentary debate, POIs are normally allowed only in the middle of a speech. The first and last minute are protected, so nobody can interrupt your opening or your wrap-up, and reply speeches are usually POI-free. Many circuits expect speakers to take a couple of POIs per speech.

How to offer one

  • Stand up briefly, say your line, and sit down if declined. Do not hover.
  • Keep it to one sentence: a sharp question or a flat counter-example.
  • Do not offer so many that you break the speaker’s flow, since judges mark that down.

How to take one

  • Accept at a natural pause, not mid-sentence.
  • Answer it in a sentence or two and return to your speech.
  • Refusing every POI looks like you are scared of the other side.

MUN uses the same name differently. In some MUN circuits a point of information is a question put to a speaker who has yielded their time to questions, and in others it is not used at all. If you are in a MUN, check how the chair uses it.

Section 10

What is adjudication, and how are debates judged?

Adjudication is the judging of a debate. The people doing it are adjudicators. A panel usually has a chair and one or two wings, and they decide the result together after the debate.

Most circuits tell adjudicators to behave like an intelligent, unbiased ordinary person. That means you judge what was said in the room, not what you personally think about the motion, and not what you happen to know that the speakers did not tell you.

Many Asian circuits score speeches on three strands:

  • Matter: what you said. The arguments, their logic, examples and relevance.
  • Manner: how you said it. Voice, pace, clarity and persuasiveness.
  • Method: how you organised it. Structure, timing, responding to the other side.

British Parliamentary adjudication is less about adding up marks and more about ranking the four teams on how well they did the job of their position. Scoring scales differ: speaker scores in some circuits centre on 75 as an average speech, in others on 70. Do not assume a number means the same thing at two different tournaments.

After the debate there is usually an oral adjudication, where the chair explains the result and gives feedback. Listen to it. It is the cheapest coaching you will ever get.

MUN judging is a different animal. Chairs score delegates across a whole committee on research, speaking, contribution to caucus and drafting, but the criteria vary from one conference to the next and are rarely published in full.

Section 11

Debate vs MUN: what’s the difference?

Both are structured argument, and both train speaking and thinking. They reward different things.

  • What you argue from. In debate you argue a side you were handed, as persuasively as you can, often with little preparation. In MUN you argue from your country’s policy and from what the UN system can actually do.
  • How it ends. A debate ends with judges choosing a winner. A MUN committee ends with a document the room adopts, often by consensus, or with no document at all.
  • Who you are up against. Debate is adversarial: there is another team, and you beat it. In MUN everyone competes for awards, but nobody gets anywhere without the rest of the room.
  • Preparation. Debate motions are often revealed shortly before the round, so you prepare by reading widely. MUN agendas are announced weeks ahead, so you prepare by researching one topic, one country and one committee in depth.
  • What wins. In debate it is clash and persuasion: why your side’s case matters more. In MUN it is understanding: how the committee works, which body can do what, and clauses with a real mechanism behind them.

Each one fixes the other’s blind spot. Debaters who try MUN learn that a brilliant argument is useless if the committee has no power to act on it. Delegates who try debate learn to find the weak point in a case and hit it hard.

At DebSoc, members can do debate, MUN or both, and MUN is optional. Nobody makes you choose in your first week.

Section 12

How to start debating in college

Almost everyone starts badly, and that is a normal first step, not a reason to wait. Here is how to start at DebSoc.

  1. Come to a DebSoc session. They run weekly in term, usually after classes. Just turn up.
  2. Join the DebSoc WhatsApp group and register on this website. Whenever an event comes up, you will get a notification.
  3. Go to the sessions when you can. Weekly practice does more than any amount of reading.
  4. Show up to external events. When another college runs a debate or a MUN, you will hear about it. Go. This is where you learn the most, because you meet people you have never seen debate before, and a lot of them are very good.
  5. Ask for feedback from everyone worth asking. That means the good debaters you meet, and the judges who mark you. Pick one thing to fix per round, not ten.

The first round will be uncomfortable. People forget their points, interrupt too early and panic during rebuttals. The way through is to go back the next week and do it again.

Section 13

Indian Parliament simulations: how a Lok Sabha committee works

A Lok Sabha committee simulates the lower house of India’s Parliament. Delegates play Members of Parliament, often real, named MPs from different parties, and the committee is run by a presiding officer who plays the Speaker. Instead of countries negotiating a resolution, you have parties arguing over a policy question or a bill. It follows the feel of Parliament more than the UN.

Simulations borrow terms from the real house, but they simplify. Here is what the main ones mean.

  • Question Hour. In Parliament, the first hour of a sitting is given to questions to ministers. In a simulation it is often a session where members question the government benches on the agenda.
  • Zero Hour. The slot after Question Hour when members raise urgent matters of public importance. It is a convention rather than a formally scheduled item, and in simulations it is where delegates bring up issues related to the agenda. At BECON MUN 2026, Zero Hour is what kept the Lok Sabha committee on its feet.
  • Bills. A bill is a proposed law. In the real Parliament a bill is introduced, debated at length, examined clause by clause, and voted on, then sent to the other house. Simulations compress this into a debate and a vote on a draft bill or motion.

Procedure in a simulation, such as how points of order work, how long speeches run and whether a motion needs seconding, is set by that conference’s Executive Board. Do not assume the real Lok Sabha rulebook applies line by line.

The main skill is staying in character as a politician with a party line, a constituency and a record, while still making a coherent argument. Study what your MP has actually said in Parliament and in public. The committees page shows the agenda DebSoc used.

Section 14

Practice motion bank: 20 motions to argue about

Written for this page, not borrowed from any tournament. Pick a side by coin toss, give yourself 15 to 30 minutes, then speak for five to seven. Motions in “This House would…” form propose an action. “This House believes…” asks you to defend a claim.

Campus and education

  1. This House would make attendance at college lectures optional.
  2. This House would replace end-semester exams with continuous assessment.
  3. This House would require every engineering student to take a course in ethics and public policy.
  4. This House believes that college fests should be funded by the students’ union rather than by sponsors.

Technology

  1. This House would ban the use of AI to grade student essays.
  2. This House regrets the rise of short-video platforms as the main source of news for young people.
  3. This House would give citizens a legal right to have any company delete their personal data after one year.
  4. This House believes that companies that profit from open-source software should be required to pay its maintainers.

Governance and politics

  1. This House would make voting compulsory in India’s local body elections.
  2. This House would limit Members of Parliament to three terms.
  3. This House believes that anti-defection laws do more harm than good.
  4. This House would broadcast every parliamentary committee meeting live.

Economy and environment

  1. This House would tax single-use plastics at the point of sale and return the revenue to citizens as an equal payment.
  2. This House believes that Indian cities should ban private cars from their old centres.
  3. This House would put metro and rail funding ahead of new highways.
  4. This House believes that gig platforms should treat delivery workers as employees.

International affairs

  1. This House believes that wealthy countries should accept climate migrants under a binding quota.
  2. This House believes that democracies should not trade with states that jail journalists.
  3. This House would give regional bodies such as ASEAN and the African Union permanent seats on the UN Security Council.
  4. This House believes that foreign aid should be tied to measurable local outcomes rather than donor priorities.

Section 15

Glossary: MUN and debate terms

Terms vary by circuit. If a chair or adjudicator uses a word differently from here, theirs wins.

Abstain
To vote neither yes nor no on a resolution, where the rules allow it.
Adjudicator
A debate judge.
Amendment
A proposed change to a draft resolution.
Background guide
The document the chairs write to introduce a committee’s agenda.
Bloc
A group of delegates with similar goals who work together.
Caucus
A phase of discussion outside the formal speakers’ list; either moderated or unmoderated.
Chair
The person running a committee or a debate. A MUN committee may have several, called the Executive Board.
Delegate
A participant in MUN, representing a country or a person.
Consensus
Adopting a document without a vote, because nobody in the room objects.
Draft resolution
A formal, formatted proposal the committee votes on.
Extension
In British Parliamentary, a new argument brought by a closing team.
Formal-formal
In UN4MUN, the formal meeting with a speakers’ list, used for opening speeches and adopting the final document.
Formal-informal
In UN4MUN, chaired but looser discussion on parts of the agenda or the draft.
GSL
General Speakers’ List, the main queue of speakers in a MUN committee.
Informal-informal
In UN4MUN, unchaired time for lobbying, negotiating and writing.
Matter, manner, method
The three strands used to score a speech in many Asian circuits: content, delivery and structure.
Moderated caucus
In UNA-USA-style procedure, a timed, chair-run discussion on a narrow topic.
Motion (debate)
The topic being debated.
Motion (MUN)
A formal request for the committee to take an action, such as starting a caucus.
Operative clause
A numbered clause in a resolution that states an action the committee will take.
Point of order
A claim that the chair or a speaker has broken the rules of procedure.
POI
Point of Information: a short question offered to a speaker, which they may accept or decline.
Position paper
A short document stating a delegation’s stance on the agenda.
Preambulatory clause
An opening clause in a resolution giving background and reasons.
Question Hour
The part of a parliamentary sitting devoted to questions to ministers.
Rebuttal
Direct response to an argument made by the other side.
Signatory
A delegate who signs a working paper to support it being discussed, without necessarily endorsing it.
Sponsor
A main author of a working paper or draft resolution.
Unmod
Short for unmoderated caucus, in UNA-USA-style procedure: free-form negotiation time.
Whip
In parliamentary debate, the final speaker on a team, who summarises and does not add new arguments. In politics, the person who enforces party discipline.
Working paper
An early, informal draft of ideas that blocs write before a draft resolution.
Yield
To hand your remaining speaking time to the chair, another delegate or questions.
Zero Hour
In the Indian Parliament, the period after Question Hour for raising urgent matters without prior notice.
